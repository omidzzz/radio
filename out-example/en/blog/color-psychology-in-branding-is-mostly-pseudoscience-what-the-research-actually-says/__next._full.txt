A separate, frequently cited paper by Lauren Labrecque and George Milne, published in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science in 2012, mapped hue against brand personality traits across a series of controlled studies and did find real correlations — red does associate with excitement, blue does associate with competence and reliability — but the authors were explicit that hue alone is a blunt instrument, and that saturation and brightness meaningfully shift how any given hue is perceived.28:T412,That saturation and brightness point turns out to matter more than most branding advice acknowledges. A 2023 study by Marco Pichierri found that lower saturation colors were consistently perceived as more eco-friendly, regardless of the specific hue involved, which is a genuinely useful and testable finding for any brand positioning itself around sustainability. Separately, Uma Bagchi and Amar Cheema's 2013 study in the same journal found that a red background specifically affected consumers' willingness to pay in negotiation contexts — but through a mechanism tied to red's association with danger and competitive arousal in that specific situation, not because red universally 'means' anything fixed across every context it appears in.29:T458,Red reads as danger, urgency, or a warning signal across much of Western branding folklore. White, coded as purity, simplicity, and cleanliness in most Western minimalist branding, is traditionally associated with mourning and funerals in large parts of East and South Asia. These aren't minor regional footnotes — they're direct contradictions of the supposedly universal emotional meaning assigned to the same hue, and they show up specifically because color meaning is learned through cultural association and repeated exposure, not hardwired into human perception the way infographic culture implies.
